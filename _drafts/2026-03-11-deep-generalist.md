# Deep Generalists: Your Time Has Come

For a few years now, I've been having a recurring discussion with friends in the tech industry. Typically framed in the realm of how systems are built, I was dying on the hill that the writing of code is just an implementation detail. It's the final step in a process that spends most of its intellectual energy in hours, days, and weeks of discussions (and/or arguments) planning, problem solving, and refining to turn an idea into an architecture and a plan. And let me tell you, my longsuffering friends who were software developers definitely let me know that they didn't agree with my premise. I think that now, in the heat of GenAI moment, it's clear that I was right: The heyday of the Deep Generalist has arrived.

I'm sure some folks are already starting to feel their blood pressure rise. I'm not here to tell you that software development is dead or that AI is taking your jobs or any other garden-variety "pRoVoCaTiVe HoT tAkE" content we are all typically seeing on LinkedIn. You can't vibe code production systems with ChatGPT and that's not changing for the foreseeable future. Seasoned, experienced software developers and engineers are irreplaceable. You can unclench your jaws.

Like I mentioned, this isn't a new sentiment for me. As varying technological developments and trends have turned infrastructure into code, data access into APIs, and made security everyone's job, the necessity of expanding knowledge outside of a specific domain has always been there, even before AI made it obvious. I think of some of the most impactful Deep Generalists I know of, folks like Kelsey Hightower, Liz Fong-Jones, or Jessie Frazelle. All of them started in one area, whether IT support like Kelsey, system administration like Liz, or front end development, like Jessie, and expanded their knowledge across domains and thrived in the junctions between them to become the renowned technologists they are today. This has been an established pattern that the current state of AI adoption has only accelerated.

Today, the expertise of the Deep Generalist is necessary not only to put AI-assisted applications into production, but to stitch together the systems necessary to guardrail and monitor those systems, train models and agents, and coalesce various types of data into accessible infrastructure. They can act as the seasoned conductor of highly skilled instrumentalists when designing and implementing these systems, especially at the velocities that AI enables for development. And like that seasoned conductor, that depth and breadth of knowledge doesn't come easily or quickly.

### Jack of All Trades or the Ace of Spades?

Some of the shade that's been thrown at the notion of Deep Generalists is the phrase "Jack of all trades, master of none". And while that's a very common colloquialism, it was coined by a certified Hater. The second half of the phrase "master of none" was added to the first part "Jack of all trades" long after it was initially coined. The original intent for Jack of all trades was as a compliment. A person of varied knowledge and experience or a so-called Renaissance Man. If you want to seem positively insufferable to people at parties, you can even use the term polymath. But none of those terms accurately describe a Deep Generalist. That is something more specific and harder to build.

The industry has tried to define this type of expertise before, whether it's the T-shaped professional with one deep vertical and broad awareness across others, the Pi-shaped variant with two, or Martin Fowler's [Expert Generalist](https://martinfowler.com/articles/expert-generalist.html). These mental models are useful, but still don't describe a Deep Generalist. Think of it as the difference between a canyon and a valley. The Deep Generalist doesn't have one or two deep areas of expertise. They have several, and they are related to each other and specifically, it's _in_ how those areas relate to each other that the true value of the Deep Generalist shines through.

The Deep Generalist isn't some mythical omniscient unicorn (well, not all of them, at least) and their knowledge isn't mastery of trivia or syntax; it's a mastery of the practical. They are experts in their ecosystem with deep understanding not only in how various systems and components function, but how they function in context with each other and the system as a whole. It's in those connections between systems and components where things break and where the Deep Generalist earns their keep.

So how does one become a Deep Generalist? Sad as I am to say, there are no certifications you can earn or a reading list to check off to amass this knowledge. It only comes from building things, watching them break, and being accountable for fixing them across multiple layers. Now, it may come as a shock to anyone who knows me for me to make a reference to BJJ, but this path mirrors progress from white belt to black belt. As I discussed in a [blog I wrote earlier this year](https://timbanks.github.io/tech/2026/02/10/ai_bjj.html), there is no shortcut to black belt. Time on the mats, training and competing with a variety of opponents with a variety of styles is the only way to gain the experience necessary to earn your black belt. The expertise of the Deep Generalist is built the same way, through time working the novel problems at seams of core components and systems and there are no  substitutes for that "mat time".

So what impact does all that mat time make when a Deep Generalist is in the room? Their impact is felt most in system and architectural design and in fixing problems with existing designs. In the design phase (which I like to call the whiteboard phase), they're asking the questions that others didn't think to ask. They're explaining the ramifications of architectural decisions before they are implemented. They're using previous failures across domains to increase the resilience, performance, security, and cost optimization of designs on the whiteboard before the first finger touches the keyboard. When they're examining existing systems, they're looking at problems from a different viewpoint and can help pull back the layers of a system to find the root of an issue. They don't replace specialists, they enable and empower them by not just explaining one domain to another, but reframing problems in a way that makes them solvable by the right people. They connect the knowledge of specialists in various domains in the same way that the conductor I mentioned earlier connects the 2nd violins with the 1st clarinets.

Picture it, Silicon Valley, March 2000. Santana and Rob Thomas were still dominating the airwaves, and you were seeing low-rise jeans in every mall. The NASDAQ was beginning to slide, but the party hadn't ended...yet. Soon, the dot-com bust was in full swing, talented IT professionals were being pink-slipped left and right, and the expensive enterprise software that had powered the frenzy quickly became a financial liability nobody could afford. Instead, the open source phoenix rose from the ashes and the [LAMP stack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LAMP_(software_bundle)) became the bedrock of Web 2.0.

The thing to understand about the LAMP stack around this time is that you had to have a handle on _every_ aspect of the ecosystem from the cylinders and sectors of the drives on the servers to whether or not the icon was cornflower blue. You were either laying hands on physical pieces of hardware or using remote connections to a BIOS to do initial hardware configuration. You were installing bootloaders and custom-compiling Linux kernels. You had to know network routing and where your default gateways were, CIDRs and subnets, configuring firewalls and iptables. On top of that you had to configure DNS. Not just `/etc/hosts` but running services like BIND locally and maintaining local and remote DNS replication. You needed to understand user accounts and permissions and jail shells. When virtualization became a thing, you had to be on top of resource allocation and automation of VPS or container creation. Logging and monitoring had to be configured and _then_ you had to have a handle on how to fix any/all of that when something went wrong.

Then comes Apache, the web server and all of its fun little quirks. Virtual hosts, `.htaccess` rewrite rules and custom domains, and making sure that all the necessary modules were loaded in the service and web root directories. Add to that reverse proxies, caching, and the various ways to generate dynamic content (we will get more into that in a second) and then, of course, still managing to make sure the DNS you configured earlier reflects the domains that you're hosting in httpd.

MySQL was often the most fraught with peril. It was the most resource intensive and the most complicated part of the stack. It had its own service configurations, its own security and permissions, its own set of tuning parameters that directly accessed resources on the operating system. It had its own language that you had to know not only to store and retrieve data, but to do administrative tasks, debug, and tune it. You had to back it up separately from everything else on the system. Snapshots and transaction logs had to be preserved to make sure any point in time could be restored from in case (and most certainly _when_) someone screwed the database up and you had to recover it.

And finally, PHP (or Perl, or Python, or Ruby, or, etc) was the application layer which used everything else underneath it to create the content that users were interacting with. And regardless of whether it was an established content management system like Wordpress, Drupal, or Joomla, storefront software like Magento, or something that someone custom-coded, you had to have a handle on how the software worked (and how it stopped working), what it was supposed to be doing, and what it was supposed to look like. This involved debugging errors in logs, troubleshooting queries, permissions issues, and UI problems. You also had to manage the security footprint of these applications to make sure they didn't become open doors to give a random person keys to the kingdom. This included cross site scripting, SQL injection, session handling, and so on.

Nobody handed you a job description that said "own everything from bare metal to the application layer." You owned it because the work demanded it; because a slow page load could be a PHP problem, a MySQL problem, a Linux I/O problem, or an Apache misconfiguration, and the only way to find out was to understand _all_ of it. And if that sounds like a lot, just imagine what job interview questions were like.

It's important to understand that the "LAMP" part of the stack wasn't exhaustive. New technologies and components landed at the seams of the stack and had to be stitched in. NGINX for reverse proxies. Varnish and Memcache for caching and/or session management. HAProxy for load balancing. All of these had to be woven together to create a seamless experience for the user and could only be done by someone with an understanding of how they work both independently and with each other. And this is the true gift of the Deep Generalist: the body of their expertise is not static. It grows as new technologies are added at the seams because the patterns of usage and interoperation are the same, even if the particular syntax is not. By the time Kubernetes came on the scene, the Deep Generalist had already integrated the use of Virtuozzo, VMWare, LXC, and Docker in the application ecosystem. The Deep Generalist learns these new domains in the context of the systems with which they integrate and because of that, they are able to find familiar patterns in new tools, along with new problems that come with them.

The thing is, there was no special recognition of the capabilities of the Deep Generalist at this time. They were just called operators or Linux systems administrators. It was _expected_ that you had an understanding of all these things. It was table stakes just to get the job. And as the sun set on Web 2.0 and the dawn of the cloud-native era was upon us, we had a whole new set of tools, processes, and methodologies, but the same patterns that the Deep Generalist was already familiar with. The names may have changed, but the pager still went off at 3am.

Today, the notion of running a LAMP stack in a datacenter feels as modern as using your rotary telephone to order pants from the Sears catalog. Now organizations are primarily using combinations of cloud-based SaaS, API-driven infrastructure from hyperscalers, and a diabolically dizzying distribution of data tools from Airflow to Zeppelin. We don't think about a _server_ anymore, instead we are thinking about things in the context of a _service_. We aren't ssh-ing into boxes we bought a rack at a time to configure them with a zip file full of bash scripts we've cobbled together over the years. Now it just takes a credit card and a few minutes to spin up enough infrastructure and services to deploy whatever shower inspiration we had ("It's like Uber for pickling onions!")

But don't let the ease of consuming services and infrastructure in the modern landscape deceive you into thinking that the underlying layers are just as effortless. The orchestration of compute, data ingest, transformation, storage, security, and observability into all those various components is a harrowing undertaking. Compute comes in flavors that can be as large as giant bare metal instances all the way down to tiny ephemeral serverless functions that just return a code and expire. You can pull in data with any number of discrete or streaming methods, transform it in countless ways, and store it as anything from a flat object in S3 to a node in a graph database. All of these services and infrastructural components emit so many metrics and logs that need to be ingested, analyzed, and visualized. DevOps has given way to GitOps, MLOps and all manner of orchestration tools to weave all these layers together into the tapestry of modern cloud architecture, and someone has to understand how all of it fits together: not just within each layer, but across all of them simultaneously.

This complexity also lends itself to even more fracturing of specialties and domains to the point that Conway's Law (the observation that systems reflect the communication structures of the organizations that build them) feels like less of a software development concept and more like a universal constant. Over 15 years of DevOps has automated away the collaboration that it was supposed to foster and communication is relegated to passive-aggressive emojis in GitHub pull requests. And while it's easy to point to this as a cultural problem within the org, the depth of the complexity and velocity of iteration drives the need to have a narrow focus to catch all the minutiae. But as more seams have developed, there are more cracks for things to fall through. Quirky data in your BI tool? The business analyst traces it to the data coming out of the ETL pipeline. The data engineer tracks it down to a schema change in the database. That schema changed due to new features being added by software engineering on the product team. Not only does the Deep Generalist help you relate how the tornado in the BI tool was caused by the butterfly flapping its wings in the product team, but they also will help you predict that effect before it happens.

Make no mistake, the modern stack isn't just the LAMP stack with a great handbag and matching Italian pumps. It's an entirely different and inherently more complex platform with more layers in more places and far more surface area to contend with. That means wider seams, more failure domains and a blast radius that puts a gigantic magnifying glass on any architectural oversights. So what could make this already-harrowing situation even better? Let's turn the velocity to 11 with AI.

Now, remember how I said before that writing code is just an implementation detail? Far be it from me to be the kind of person to say "I told you so", but I'm a Virgo, so I'm just going to show you the receipts and stare at you. According to [Stack Overflow](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/ai#sentiment-and-usage-ai-select-ai-sel-prof) just over half of professional developers use AI tools to write code every day. According to [JetBrains](https://blog.jetbrains.com/research/2025/10/state-of-developer-ecosystem-2025/), 62% of developers rely on at least one AI coding assistant, agent, or code editor. Notice they're saying developers are _using_ AI, it's not _replacing_ them (unless you work at Block). The actual writing of the code is becoming a more trivial undertaking because that's never where the value in software development was.

And let's face it, GenAI is _really_ good at generating code when given ample direction and context. It's good at explaining what something is doing, catching some commonalities or anomalies, and accelerating the efforts of developers overall. But that utility starts to fall off when GenAI needs to reason across novel situations or the ambiguous, undocumented seams of the landscape between systems. That won't stop it from trying to be genuinely helpful, though, and you can trust it to write a perfectly good solution to a question you didn't ask when it doesn't have sufficient understanding of a system to know what to do. This is where vibe coding gets folks into trouble. You can prompt your way into creating a fantastic looking React artifact of whatever functionality you asked for, but just like the difference between a papier mache car and a real car, it's going to fall apart as soon as you try to take it on the road.
